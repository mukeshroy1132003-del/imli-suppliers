Whole, seeded or seedless: which tamarind should you buy?

Whole pods store longest and cost least per kilo, but you lose 40–45% of the weight to shell and seed. Seeded blocks are the everyday choice for home kitchens. Seedless blocks cost more upfront and save labour in any kitchen that processes more than a few kilos a day.

For a hotel using 10 kg of extract a week, seedless usually works out cheaper once soaking and straining time is counted.

Why tamarind prices move between February and May

Read article

Most Indian tamarind is harvested from late January to May. Prices are lowest at peak arrival and rise through the monsoon as stocks shrink.

Buyers who book their annual requirement in March and store it cool usually pay the least over the year.

How to store tamarind blocks for 12 months

Read article

Keep blocks off the floor, away from walls and out of direct sun. Moisture is the main enemy, so store in a dry room and keep the film sealed.

In very humid cities, cold storage at 4–10 °C from June to September keeps colour and texture.

Paste or block: what works better in a commercial kitchen?

Read article

Paste saves time and gives the same strength every time, which helps when several cooks follow one recipe. Blocks cost less per kilo of sourness.

Many restaurants use paste for chutneys and blocks for sambar and curries.

The market for tamarind seeds

Read article

Tamarind kernel powder is used in textiles, paper and food as a thickener. That keeps steady demand for clean, dry seed.

Seed buyers look for low moisture and no pulp residue, so cleaning matters as much as quantity.
